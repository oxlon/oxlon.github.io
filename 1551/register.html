Requirement register

#1What this register is

The specification states twelve functional requirements in twelve sentences, and a sentence is not something a delivery can be checked against. Each requirement was therefore reproduced verbatim in its original wording, decomposed into its atomic sub-demands, and each sub-demand traced twice: to the Ministry data that serves it — file, sheet, row or series code, coverage years — and to where the module implements it, by notebook and by series code. That produces 58 atomic rows across the 12 requirements, which is the table below. It exists to be checked against, not to argue for the module, and nothing in it is softened for presentation.

Two structural findings came out of the exercise and are recorded here rather than buried in a row note. First, the specification's own numbering does not track the decree's clause numbering: three decree clauses — average monthly wages, employment and paid services to the population — have no functional requirement of their own anywhere in the list, although the statutory template carries their rows. They are kept under the income requirement for practical reasons and labelled as what they are. Second, three of the twelve requirements are the same request read more than once: the trade and balance-of-payments requirements overlap by construction, which is why one notebook serves two of them and two notebooks jointly serve a third.

#2The standard applied

Three statuses are used, and the boundary between them is deliberately unkind to us.

TAM (complete)
— the sub-demand is served: the quantity is published for the required period, it comes from an equation or an identity that the module owns, and where it is behavioural it has a row in the backtest ledger.
QİSMƏN (partial)
— the quantity is published but something the requirement implies is missing, and the row says exactly what. The strict part of the rule lives here: a quantity obtained only by identity — a residual with no equation of its own and no backtest row — is scored partial even though the number exists and is internally consistent. The same test is applied to every such case, so that the standard cannot be relaxed for one requirement and enforced on another.
BOŞLUQ (gap)
— the quantity is not served. A gap is only recorded after a negative proof: concept-first searches in all three languages of the source material, with cell-level re-reads of the candidate sheets, separating what was found from what is confirmed missing. Where the negative proof holds, the row names the data request that would close it.

Status is assigned to the sub-demand, not to the requirement, so a requirement can be complete in most of its parts and carry a gap in one. The scoreboard is therefore 42 complete, 14 partial and 2 gaps out of 58 — not twelve verdicts on twelve requirements.

One caveat belongs on this page. On the build date, 6 recorded reasons were re-opened against the live output files: rows FR1.k, FR1.l, FR2.d, FR2.e, FR3.d, FR4.a–g and FR12.d. In no case did the verdict change; in most of them the stated reason or the counts did, because work completed after the row was written had not been carried back into it. Where the note in the table and the live artefacts differ, the requirement's own page carries the re-derived reading and identifies the difference.
